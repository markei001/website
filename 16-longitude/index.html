An essay in five distances

The Slow Light

Everything overhead is a postcard, and some of the postcards are very old. A short walk outward, with a return ticket.

about 1,800 words/about 8 minutes/Notes in the margin

Step outside on a clear night and let your eye settle on whatever it likes. Wherever it lands, you are not looking at the thing itself. You are reading a letter about it, and the letter has been in the post.

This is not a figure of speech. Light travels at 299,792,458 metres per second,1The number is that exact because it is a definition. Since 1983 the metre has been defined as the distance light covers in 1/299,792,458 of a second. The speed of light is a decree now; the metre is what gets adjusted. which sounds like plenty until you try to cross anything astronomical with it. Then it is slow the way a ferry is slow: dependable, finite, and a nuisance if you were hoping to arrive. Every glint overhead set out some time ago, and how long ago depends on how far away it sits. So the sky is not one picture. It is a stack of pictures of different ages, pinned to the same black sheet.

I · 1.3 seconds ago

The Moon, or: light has a speed

For most of history nobody thought light travelled at all. It simply was, everywhere at once. Galileo doubted this enough to test it, with an assistant, two shuttered lanterns and a pair of hills a mile apart.2Light crosses a mile in about five microseconds. A person’s reaction time is around a fifth of a second, forty thousand times longer. The experiment measured the assistant. He found nothing, and said so: if light was not instantaneous, it was at least very quick.

The break came in Paris in 1676, from a Dane named Ole Rømer who was timing eclipses of Io, the innermost of Jupiter’s big moons. Io makes a good clock, slipping into Jupiter’s shadow every forty-two hours or so. Rømer saw that the eclipses ran early while the Earth swung toward Jupiter and late while it swung away. The clock wasn’t wrong; the signal was taking longer because the road was longer. Light had a speed, and his rough figure of 220,000 kilometres per second was only a quarter short, which is rather good for 1676a.

The Moon is the gentlest introduction. At its average distance of 384,400 kilometres, its light reaches you in 1.28 seconds. Apollo crews left reflectors there, and observatories still fire lasers at them; the photons return about two and a half seconds later, and from many such trips we know the Moon is leaving us at 3.8 centimetres a year, the speed of a fingernail. You would never notice a lag that small, but it is the first rung of an argument: what you see is not happening in the tense you see it. The Moon is a second old, which makes it close to the youngest thing in the night sky. A satellite is younger by a few thousandths of a second and will not be interested.

II · 8 minutes 19 seconds ago

The Sun, and a longer trip than it looks

Sunlight takes 499 seconds to cross 149.6 million kilometres. That is eight minutes and nineteen seconds. If the Sun were switched off, you would go on sitting in daylight for those eight minutes, and then in the dark. The Earth would also go on orbiting a Sun that was no longer there for exactly as long, because changes in gravity travel at the speed of light too.3General relativity predicts it, and in 2017 two neutron stars merging gave a test. Gravitational waves and gamma rays set out together from some 130 million light-years away and arrived within about two seconds of each other. The news simply takes that long to travel.

That is the cheap part of the journey, though. Sunlight is made by fusion in the core, and from there it cannot leave in a straight line. The interior is so dense that a photon is absorbed and re-emitted countless times, shoved in a random direction each time, and it staggers outward like someone looking for the exit in the dark. Published estimates of how long this takes run from about ten thousand years to well over a hundred thousand. So the warmth on your face left the Sun’s surface eight minutes ago, but the energy in it was set loose around when people began to farm, by the low estimate, or long before anyone painted a cave, by the high one. It is the oldest light most of us will ever feel, and it feels like an afternoon.

III · 4.2 years ago

The neighbours, and a unit that isn’t about time

“Light-year” is the most misused word in popular astronomy. It is a distance, not a duration: 9.46 trillion kilometres, the road light covers in a year.

The nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri, sits 4.24 light-years away. It is a faint red dwarf, invisible to the naked eye, and the light you would see left about four years and three months ago. Sirius is 8.6 years behind. Vega is about 25: if you are reading this in the 2020s, the Vega overhead tonight is the Vega of the turn of the millennium.

Distances like these were first won by parallax. In 1838 Friedrich Bessel watched a faint, fast-moving star in Cygnus swing against the background as the Earth went round the Sun,4Close one eye, hold up a finger, switch eyes: the finger jumps against the wall. Same trick, with a baseline of 300 million kilometres. and from the swing he got roughly ten light-years, against the 11.4 we accept today. It was the first measured gap to a star, and larger than anyone had dared guess.

Then there is Betelgeuse, the red shoulder of Orion, 550 to 700 light-years away depending on whose measurement you prefer. In late 2019 it dimmed to its faintest in the modern record and a minor panic went round that it was about to explode. What we were watching had set off in the thirteen- or fourteen-hundreds. (It turned out the star had coughed out a cloud of dust that veiled it from the frontb. No explosion.) Whether it has done anything since, nobody alive can say; the news is on the road, though a few centuries are a rounding error in the life of a red supergiant.

This is worth saying plainly, since the popular version has gone a little too far: the stars you see are almost all still there. Nearly everything with a name is within a few thousand light-years,5The farthest of the bright naked-eye stars is probably Deneb, in Cygnus: somewhere between 1,400 and 2,600 light-years off, depending on whose parallax you trust. Even its lower figure is older than most countries. and stars die slowly. You are not mostly looking at ghosts. You are looking at a set of portraits, each painted at a different time, hung in the same room.

IV · 2.5 million years ago

Andromeda, the farthest you can see unaided

The farthest thing most people will ever see without help is a smudge. From somewhere properly dark, on an autumn evening in the northern hemisphere, find the zigzag of Cassiopeia and follow its arrow toward the south, and you may catch a faint, elongated blur: the Andromeda galaxy, a trillion stars blended into the glow of a thin cloud. Its light left about 2.5 million years ago, when someone in East Africa was learning to knock an edge onto a stone. If our eyes were better the galaxy would cover six full Moons.6Six Moons, drawn to scale. Its neighbour, the Triangulum galaxy, is a little farther at 2.7 million light-years, and is supposed to be visible too, under a very dark sky. People report it the way they report sea monsters.

How do we know how far away it is? You need a candle whose wattage you know: compare its true brightness with how dim it looks, and the dimming is a ruler. In 1912 Henrietta Leavitt, who spent her days at Harvard measuring other people’s photographic plates, noticed that among the pulsing Cepheid stars of the Small Magellanic Cloud, the slower a star pulses the brighter it really isc. In 1923 Edwin Hubble found one in photographs of Andromeda, still often thought of as a “nebula” inside our own galaxy, and the distance that dropped out put it far beyond the Milky Way’s edged. His number was about a third of today’s, which hardly mattered. There were other galaxies, and the universe had just got very much bigger across a few photographic plates.

V · 13.5 billion years ago

Reading it

The trouble with starlight is that it arrives with no return address. A faint galaxy delivers a few photons a minute to a mirror the size of a lorry. Pass sunlight through a prism and the rainbow is crossed by dark lines, hundreds of them. In 1859 Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen showed that each line belongs to a chemical element, which meant the Sun’s composition could be read from 150 million kilometres away. Helium was spotted in the solar spectrum in 1868 and named for Helios; it was not found on Earth for nearly thirty years. An element had been discovered in the sky before anyone looked in a cupboard.

The lines also move. Light from something receding is stretched toward the red, and in the 1910s Vesto Slipher found that nearly every spiral “nebula” he measured had its lines shifted that way. By the end of the 1920s Georges Lemaître and Hubble had the pattern: the farther the galaxy, the bigger the shift, because space itself grows while the light is crossing it and stretches the waves along with it.7Hydrogen’s lines, as emitted and as received after the light’s wavelengths have grown by 15 per cent. Every line moves, and the longer ones move farthest. Redshift is therefore a speed and a clock at once. Wavelengths are stretched by a factor of one plus the redshift, z.

The record keeps being broken, but as I write, galaxies with z beyond fourteen have been confirmed by the James Webb Space Telescopee. Take one: its ultraviolet hydrogen line, 121.6 nanometres when it left, has been pulled out fifteenfold to about 1.9 micrometres. That is infrared, which is why the telescope was built to see infrared. The galaxy’s light has travelled for about 13.5 billion years, setting out when the universe was some 290 million years old..8The galaxy itself is now far farther off than 13.5 billion light-years, since the space between us kept growing while the light crossed it. Travel time and distance are different questions, and the universe is not obliged to make them agree.

Behind all the galaxies lies the oldest light there is, the cosmic microwave background, released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang when the universe first cooled enough to turn transparent. It set out as a glow at roughly 3,000 kelvin, and has been stretched about eleven-hundredfold into microwaves at 2.7 kelvin. A small fraction of the hiss between channels on an old analogue television was that light.

Nobody here has a time machine. What astronomers have is an archive filed by distance. To see how galaxies grow up you cannot watch one for a billion years, so you collect one at each age, like a school hall’s photographs of six-year-olds and sixteen-year-olds, and assume, with some argument, that they are all the same kind of child.

VI · Now, or whatever that is

Back to now

Here is the awkward part. All this talk of lateness assumes an “on time” to be late against: a shared now across the universe, in which Andromeda is up to something at this moment and the report is delayed. Relativity says there is no such thing. Two people in the same room, one sitting still and the other walking across it, will disagree about which events in Andromeda happened “at the same time” as their lunch, by several days9The log-scaled ladder this essay has just climbed. Each step is a thousand times or so further down than the last, which is why the ticker in the header goes quickly and then not at all. Neither is wrong. There is no fact for either of them to be wrong about.

That is Roger Penrose’s puzzlef, and it loosens the idea of delay. The sky is not a late broadcast of some live event out of sight. Whatever “is happening now” in Andromeda cannot touch you until its light, or something slower, arrives; for every purpose that matters to a person, that arrival is when it happens. The light is not behind the event. It is the event, reaching you.

I find this steadying rather than sad. Go back out and look up. The Moon a second old, the Sun’s long gold detour, Vega from the year 2000, Betelgeuse from the age of manuscripts, the smudge from before our species had a name for itself. All of it at different ages and all of it accurate, hung in one dark room by a very slow lamp. Nothing you can see tonight is current. All of it is true.
